This is the unforced version of the Millennium Prize existence-and-smoothness problem and would be stronger than a forced blow-up example. The supplied material does not include the mathematical proof or independent verification, so the result remains an unverified claim.

hackernews · trotro · Nov 20, 15:34 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50576232)

**「Why the unforced case matters」** The Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem asks whether smooth solutions can develop a singularity in finite time. Community comments describe an earlier OpenAI result as using a carefully designed external force, which does not settle the unforced case.

**「Verification status」** Until the argument is checked by independent mathematicians, it should be treated as a preprint claim rather than an established theorem. If verified, it would establish that global smooth solutions do not exist for some initial data and would require reassessing regularity assumptions used in fluid-dynamics modeling.

**「Community reaction」** Commenters describe the unforced result as more realistic than the earlier forced OpenAI example, while one commenter calls the day historically significant because it reportedly coincides with a generalized Riemann Hypothesis result. Others ask for a physical or numerical demonstration that makes the blow-up behavior intuitive.

The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis is a conjecture about the location of the nontrivial zeros of Dirichlet L-functions, extending the classical Riemann Hypothesis. Lean is a dependently-typed proof assistant used to formally verify mathematical proofs, and prior work such as Buckmaster&\#x27;s finite-time blow-up results had already advanced the boundary of what could be established about Navier-Stokes behavior.

**「Mathematicians can independently check the proofs」** For the mathematical community, the practical consequence is that these results are checkable rather than merely assertable. Tao notes that for the first two results the digestion path is already visible — a human-readable proof with a broadly clear strategy, with the Lean formalisation layered on top — so a reader can form a judgement in an afternoon. **「Community concerns」** Commenters focused on whether formal verification guarantees the intended theorem: one asked how to ensure a large Lean development proves the right statement rather than a trivial one, while another noted that acceptance by two independent kernels reduces the chance of exploiting a kernel bug. Others expressed concern that AI is extending existing mathematical abstractions rather than creating new ones, and several described career uncertainty or fear about the future of human research.

### [Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Mini open-weight looped transformer](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Thinking Machines has released Inkling 2 Mini, an open-weight model built on a looped-transformer architecture and aimed at local deployment. The model is trained end-to-end in nvfp4, and its sizing was reportedly chosen based on what local-inference users most often request. Community reports describe it as competitive with Qwen3.8-27B, but these are user benchmarks rather than independently verified results, and the supplied evidence is partial.

hackernews · jasondavies · Nov 20, 20:12 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50581260)

**「Background」** A looped transformer reuses the same layer stack across multiple passes rather than stacking distinct layers, which can reduce the parameter count needed for a given depth. Open-weight models are distributed with their weights published so users can run and modify them locally, typically through inference stacks such as llama.cpp or Ollama.

**「Impact」** Local users can run Inkling 2 Mini on consumer hardware, with community testers reporting roughly 27 tokens/s on a Ryzen 395 via llama.cpp Vulkan and about 10 tokens/s on a 32GB M1 Pro MacBook at q4 quantization. Practical use still depends on unmerged llama.cpp pull requests and a small patch for a reported ~30% speed boost, so performance is not yet stable out of the box.

**「Community discussion」** Commenters are split: several report strong coding results and speculate that open models will increasingly adopt looped architectures, while one user describes a test where the model failed and produced thousands of tokens of irrelevant output. These are individual experiences and opinions, not established facts about the model&\#x27;s reliability.

### [METR&\#x27;s updated time horizon plot shows exponential gains but judgment gaps](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/11/20/1149399/the-most-misunderstood-graph-in-ai-just-got-a-major-update/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

METR, the AI evaluation nonprofit, published a Q4 2026 capability update that revises its widely shared time horizon plot, which originally showed the length of tasks frontier models could complete doubling roughly every four months. The updated plot adds more tasks, including some estimated to take humans far longer and others that are more open-ended, and it finds that exponential capability gains continue on verifiable work while models still fall short of humans on judgment-heavy tasks. On one new task, models designed AI evaluations for METR to use, and not one was judged useful by METR employees; on a philosophical-argument scoring task, models also fell short of human performance. The newest models, such as Opus 5.5, now reach what METR calls &quot;borderline above the bar&quot; on evaluation design, whereas a year earlier every model scored &quot;clearly below the bar,&quot; though METR includes few such tasks and says trends on them will not be as clean as for easy-to-specify ones.

rss · AI News \(MIT Tech Review\) · Nov 20, 18:15

**「Why the plot matters」** METR&\#x27;s time horizon plot scores task difficulty by how long a human takes to complete a task, then rates models by the length of tasks they can finish; it was widely shared and, per MIT Technology Review&\#x27;s earlier coverage, widely misinterpreted because of the complexity of its measures. The original suite excluded open-ended, judgment-laden tasks because they are hard to evaluate, even though reinforcement learning works best on verifiable tasks like coding and math, where success can be checked automatically.

**「What this means for evaluators」** For anyone using the time horizon plot to gauge progress toward automating AI research and development, the update weakens the difficulty calibration: unlike the original plot, where human completion times were measured by actually having people attempt each task, the new report&\#x27;s completion times are mostly estimates because paying people for tasks that could take hundreds of hours is prohibitively expensive. METR&\#x27;s own staff say this makes it hard to establish trends as robust as those for easy-to-specify tasks, so the judgment-heavy results should be read as a current snapshot rather than a reliable extrapolation.

### [Alpöge Claims Formalized Proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Levent Alpöge has published a note claiming he has found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, with a method he says extends to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis \(GRH\) and, given more time, the Extended Riemann Hypothesis. He reports working with Anthropic&\#x27;s Claude and states that the arguments have been formalized &quot;without assumptions&quot; in Lean. The formalization is distributed as 14 compressed parts totaling about 1.3 GB and roughly 15 million lines of Lean code. The claim is not yet verified by the mathematical community, and a separate GRH claim by Alessandro Masullo is circulating at the same time.

hackernews · iamronaldo · Nov 20, 14:16 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50574968)

**「What GRH and formal verification mean」** The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis \(GRH\) is a conjecture in analytic number theory that extends the Riemann Hypothesis — one of the Clay Millennium Prize Problems — to Dirichlet L-functions, and it underlies many conditional results in number theory. Formal verification means encoding a mathematical proof inside a proof assistant such as Lean, so that every step is machine-checked rather than reviewed by hand.

**「Independent verification is possible but still pending」** Because the argument is formalized in Lean and distributed as a downloadable codebase \(~1.3GB, roughly 15 million lines, split across 14 tar.gz parts\), anyone with a Lean toolchain can attempt to check it directly rather than relying on the author&\#x27;s word. The claim nonetheless remains unverified by the mathematical community, and a competing GRH claim by Alessandro Masullo is circulating in parallel, so both should be treated as unconfirmed until experts complete their review.

**「Community reaction」** Commenters focus on the artifact&\#x27;s scale, with one user sharing a script to download and reassemble the 14 parts and reporting roughly 15 million lines of Lean, and questioning whether such a codebase can realistically be checked. Others weigh in on the pace of AI-assisted mathematics, with one commenter arguing Anthropic should consider coordinating a pause and another noting a 2014 interview in which Alpöge called solving the Riemann Hypothesis his life goal.

### [Why Output Caching Doesn&\#x27;t Work](https://seangoedecke.com/why-output-caching-doesnt-work/) ⭐️ 5.0/10

rss · Sean Goedecke · Nov 21, 00:00

**「Background」** LLM inference economics depend on KV-cache input caching, which stops the whole conversation history from being recomputed on every message. The author asks whether the same trick could apply to model outputs, since agents visibly repeat content — code snippets, quotes from their own evidence docs, sample model outputs — and output tokens are far costlier to produce than input tokens because attention lets inputs be ingested in parallel while outputs must be generated one at a time.

**「Solution」** The tempting fix is to stop models regurgitating themselves: prompt or fine-tune a coding model to emit &quot;src/script.py:12-15&quot; instead of the actual lines, or more generally to reference segments of its own conversation history, which the harness could then expand transparently for the user. The author&\#x27;s objection is that this only optimizes visible output, and he believes models rarely repeat themselves in their internal chain of thought, since that information is already available to them. From his own agent histories, visible output is about 6% of output tokens, with 50% reasoning and 44% tool calls. So the argument runs straight into Amdahl&\#x27;s law: even saving two-thirds of visible output, which he calls a ridiculously optimistic estimate, would only cut about 4% of total output tokens. He adds that mature inference providers likely already exploit output repetition through speculative decoding on input n-grams. The real lever is the 94% of invisible tokens, which depends largely on intelligence — a smarter model needs fewer reasoning tokens and tool calls, which is why a model 5x more expensive per token can be cheaper overall if it reaches the right answer with 6x fewer tokens. He is candid about the limits of his evidence: he can&\#x27;t read frontier chains of thought, is only moderately confident they don&\#x27;t loop, and openly asks readers whether tool calls themselves repeat content composed in the chain of thought.

**「Takeaway」** Output caching is a dead end because the expensive part of agent inference is invisible reasoning and tool-call tokens, not the visible text users read. Meaningful output-token savings therefore come from model intelligence rather than deduplicating what the model says out loud.
